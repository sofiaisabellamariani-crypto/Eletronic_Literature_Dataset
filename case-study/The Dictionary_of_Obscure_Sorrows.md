## Object Description (digital literary object)

### **Origins**

*The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows* was first conceived in **2006**, when John Koenig was studying at Macalester College and attempting to write poetry. At the time, he felt that existing language lacked the emotional precision he needed. The dictionary began as a **personal creative tool**, a place to invent the words he wished already existed — especially for emotions that had never been linguistically described.  

Over time, this private experiment evolved into a public-facing digital project. Its popularity exploded in **2015**, when a list of 23 invented words circulated widely on social media platforms, transforming the site into a viral cultural phenomenon and introducing Koenig’s neologisms to a global audience.

### What it is

The work is a **born‑digital lexicon** of invented emotional terms, each accompanied by a poetic definition and, in some cases, a short video essay. It blends **neologism, micro‑essay, conceptual writing and multimodal storytelling**, creating a hybrid form that sits between dictionary, poetry and philosophical reflection.

### Where it lives

The project lives primarily on the website of [*The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows*](https://www.thedictionaryofobscuresorrows.com). While some entries include videos originally published on external platforms, the website remains the central, authoritative version of the work.

### How it is accessed

The dictionary is accessible through any modern web browser and Koenig periodically updates the site, making it a **living, evolving digital text** rather than a fixed publication.

### What counts as “the text”

- The invented words.
- Koenig’s definitions.  
- The short poetic elaborations accompanying each term.  
- The structure of the website (index, categories, navigation).  
- Multimodal entries, including video essays.  

### What does *not* count as “the text”

- User comments or social‑media circulation. 
- The 2021 print book (a derivative but distinct object).  
- External adaptations, memes, or commercial appropriations.  
- The visual layout (paratextual rather than textual).

**The “text” is therefore a web‑native modular lexicon, not a narrative.**

## Textual Model & Medium

### Linear / Non‑linear

The work is **non-linear**.
There is no set reading order: each entry is self-contained and accessible independently. It can be found by browsing by "concept", by "tag", or more orderly in the "all words" section.

### Modularity

The lexicon is **highly modular**:

- Each word is a discrete unit.
- Entries do not depend on one another. 
- The collection can expand indefinitely.  

This modularity mirrors the conceptual premise: emotions are discrete yet interconnected phenomena.

### Interactivity

Interactivity is minimal but meaningful:

- Clicking entries.
- Browsing categories.
- Occasionally watching embedded videos.  

There is no branching narrative or reader‑driven transformation of the text.

### What the interface does to reading

The interface shapes the reading experience by:

- Encouraging **sampling** rather than sequential reading.  
- Framing each entry as a **discovery**. 
- Foregrounding the text through minimal design. 
- Inviting **drifting** and associative exploration. 

The result is a reading experience that is **episodic, reflective and exploratory**.

## Taxonomies and Site Structure

One of the most distinctive features of *The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows* is its **multi‑layered taxonomic system**. The website organizes entries through overlapping classification structures — **TOC**, **Chapters**, **Tags**, **Concepts** and **All Words** — transforming the project from a static lexicon into a **dynamic conceptual database**.

- **TOC (Table of Contents)** offers a curated overview of the work.  
- **Chapters** introduce quasi‑narrative segmentation, grouping words into thematic clusters.  
- **Tags** create semantic networks, enabling associative navigation.  
- **Concepts** group entries into philosophical, psychological, or affective categories.  
- **All Words** provides a complete, non‑hierarchical list for free exploration.

These taxonomies encourage **semantic wandering** and **conceptual clustering**, highlighting the hybrid identity of the work: part dictionary, part poetic atlas, part emotional taxonomy. They also reinforce the project’s central idea — that emotions can be mapped, named and explored through multiple interconnected pathways.

## Narratology

Applying narratology to this work is challenging because *The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows* is **not narrative**. Still, narratological tools illuminate aspects of its structure.

### Narrator / Narrative Voice

The voice is:

- **Authorial**, since Koenig defines each term. 
- **Poetic**, blending dictionary precision with lyrical prose.
- **Quasi‑omniscient**, describing universal human emotions.  
- **Impersonal yet intimate**, addressing the reader directly.

There is no character‑based narrator; the voice functions as a **philosophical observer**.

### Narrative Time

There is no plot, but time appears conceptually:

- Definitions describe **atemporal** emotional states.  
- Some entries evoke temporal metaphors (nostalgia, anticipation, memory).

Classical narratological categories (order, duration, frequency) do not apply meaningfully.

### Focalization

Focalization is **external and universalized**.  
The text often addresses “you,” “we,” or humanity in general, creating a collective emotional perspective.

### Segmentation / Pacing

Segmentation is:

- **Lexical** (each entry = one unit)
- **Non‑hierarchical**  
- **Reader‑determined**  

Pacing depends entirely on the reader, who may read one entry or dozens in a single session.

### Does narratology fit?

Only partially.  
The work is a **conceptual lexicon**, not a narrative.  
Narratology helps describe voice and segmentation, but plot‑based categories are only metaphorically applicable.

## Quantitative Glimpse

A full quantitative analysis is not feasible because:

- The site is dynamic and updated.
- Scraping would violate responsible extraction guidelines.  
- Some entries include multimedia rather than extractable text.

However, manual observation reveals:

- **100+ entries** currently listed.  
- Definitions typically range **50–150 words**  
- Entries follow a consistent structure:  
  **term → definition → poetic elaboration**

This confirms the work’s **micro‑textual** and **modular** nature.

## Reflection

### Affordances (what the object makes possible)

- A **living lexicon** that can grow and reorganize itself.
- A reading experience based on **drift, browsing and emotional resonance.**  
- Integration of **multimodal elements** (video essays).  
- A conceptual space where **language invention becomes a literary act.**
- A structure that encourages **cultural circulation and reinterpretation.**

### Limits (what the object makes difficult)

- No overarching narrative. 
- Minimal interactivity.  
- Fragmentation due to modularity. 
- Versioning challenges due to ongoing updates.

### Cultural Impact

Several neologisms have entered popular culture:

- **Vemödalen** inspired discussions on photography and creativity, especially through its video essay exploring repetition and uniqueness. 
- **Sonder** has been used for album titles, brands, video games and artistic projects.

This demonstrates the work’s **memetic power** and its ability to circulate beyond its original context highlighting the project’s **linguistic creativity**, **emotional resonance** and **cultural relevance**.

### What is lost in its paper version

- The **non‑linear browsing experience.**  
- The sense of **discovery.**  
- The **multimodal** dimension (videos, animations). 
- The evolving, open‑ended nature of the project.  

**The digital form is essential to the work’s identity as a living, expanding, web‑native lexicon of emotions.**
